Module 1

Fundamentals

Every robot, from a line follower to a self-flying drone, runs the same loop. This module names its parts.

The loop: sense, think, act

A robot measures the world with sensors, decides what to do in software, and changes the world with actuators. Then it measures again. How fast that loop runs sets what the robot can do. Balancing a drone needs the loop to close hundreds of times a second. Choosing which room to drive to can take a full second.

That gap in speed is why real robots use two kinds of computer, which you will meet at the end of this module.

Just enough electricity

  • Voltage (V) is electrical pressure. Current (A) is flow. Power (W) is volts times amps.
  • Logic runs at 3.3 V or 5 V and sips milliamps. Motors run at battery voltage and gulp amps. Never power a motor from a logic pin.
  • Everything shares a common ground, or signals mean nothing.
  • Battery capacity is in amp-hours. A 5 Ah pack can supply 5 A for about an hour, or 20 A for about 15 minutes.

PWM: faking an analog signal

A digital pin is either on or off. Switch it thousands of times a second and vary the fraction of time it spends on, the duty cycle, and a motor feels the average. This is pulse-width modulation, and it drives nearly every motor and servo you will use.

No sensor is trustworthy alone. Module 3 shows how to combine two flawed ones into a good estimate.

Two kinds of computer

A microcontroller (Arduino, STM32, ESP32, or the chip inside a drone's flight controller) runs one program with no operating system in the way. It can read an encoder and update a motor at an exact rate. It cannot run a neural network on camera frames.

A single-board computer such as your Jetson Orin Nano runs Linux, has a GPU, and handles vision and planning. Linux does not promise when your code will run to the millisecond, so it is a poor choice for the innermost motor loop.

The standard design uses both: the microcontroller keeps the machine stable, the Jetson tells it where to go.

Module 2

Control

Telling a motor "go to 90 degrees" is easy. Getting it there fast, without overshooting, under a load you did not plan for, is control.

Open loop and closed loop

Open loop means you send a command and hope. Set 50% PWM and the wheel turns at some speed that changes with battery charge, slope and carpet. Closed loop means you measure the result, compare it with the target, and correct. The difference between target and measurement is the error.

PID in three sentences

  • P, proportional: push harder the further you are from the target. Too little is sluggish, too much oscillates.
  • I, integral: add up error over time, so a small leftover error eventually gets corrected. Removes the offset a constant load causes.
  • D, derivative: resist fast change. Acts like a brake as you approach the target and damps overshoot.
error      = target - measured
integral  += error * dt
derivative = (error - last_error) / dt
output     = Kp*error + Ki*integral + Kd*derivative
Try it

Tune a position controller

The target jumps from 0 to 1 at time zero. Aim for a fast rise, under 5% overshoot, and zero final error.

targetresponse

Experiments: set Kd to 0 and raise Kp until it rings. Turn on the load with Ki at 0 and watch it stop short. Then add Ki.

A tuning order that works

  1. Set Ki and Kd to zero. Raise Kp until the response is quick and just starts to oscillate.
  2. Add Kd until the oscillation dies.
  3. Add a little Ki only if a steady error remains.

On real hardware, also limit the integral so it cannot wind up while the motor is saturated, and filter the derivative because sensor noise makes it jumpy.

Loops inside loops

Robots stack controllers. On a drone, a position loop asks for a tilt angle, an angle loop asks for a rotation rate, and a rate loop commands the motors. Each inner loop runs faster than the one outside it. On a rover, a velocity loop per wheel sits under the navigation software. You will see both in module 6.

Module 3

Motion and state estimation

A robot has to know where it is and how its motor commands turn into movement. Both come down to geometry and a healthy distrust of sensors.

Frames

Every measurement is taken from somewhere. A lidar reports distances from the lidar, a camera from its lens. To use them together you express everything in shared coordinate frames. The common ones:

  • base_link: fixed to the robot body. Forward is x, left is y, up is z.
  • odom: where the robot thinks it started. Smooth, but drifts over time.
  • map: a fixed world frame. Accurate over the long run, may jump when corrected.

A transform is the rotation and translation between two frames. Getting a sensor's transform wrong by a few centimeters is one of the most common reasons a robot misbehaves.

Kinematics of a two-wheeled robot

A differential drive robot steers by spinning its wheels at different speeds. With wheel speeds vL and vR and a distance L between the wheels:

forward speed  v = (vR + vL) / 2
turn rate      w = (vR - vL) / L
Try it

Six seconds of driving

Grid squares are 1 m. Wheel spacing is 0.30 m. Try equal speeds, then equal and opposite.

Run this in reverse and you have odometry: read how far each wheel turned, and work out how the robot moved. Small errors add up with every step, so odometry alone always drifts.

Fusing two bad sensors into one good one

To measure tilt, an accelerometer senses the direction of gravity. It is right on average but shakes with every vibration. A gyro measures rotation rate, and adding it up gives a smooth angle that slowly drifts away. A complementary filter trusts the gyro in the short term and the accelerometer in the long term:

angle = a * (angle + gyro_rate * dt) + (1 - a) * accel_angle
Try it

Blend gyro and accelerometer

true angleaccelerometergyro onlyfused

At 1.00 the fused line is the drifting gyro. Near 0.50 it copies the accelerometer's noise. The best error sits in between.

The Kalman filter is the grown-up version of this idea. It tracks how uncertain each source is and weights them accordingly, moment by moment. Drone flight controllers and rover localization both run an extended Kalman filter (EKF). You will configure one, not write one.

Module 4

ROS 2

ROS 2 is the plumbing most robots share. It lets small programs written by different people pass data to each other without knowing who is on the other end.

The four ideas

  • Node: one program doing one job, such as a camera driver or a path planner.
  • Topic: a named stream of messages. Nodes publish to it or subscribe to it. Many to many.
  • Service: a quick request and reply, like "reset odometry".
  • Action: a long task with progress and cancel, like "drive to the kitchen".
Try it

A perception pipeline, tap each part

Commands you will use every day

ros2 node list                 # what is running
ros2 topic list                # what streams exist
ros2 topic echo /cmd_vel       # watch messages go by
ros2 topic hz /image_raw       # is the camera hitting its rate?
ros2 run tf2_tools view_frames # draw the frame tree
ros2 bag record -a             # record everything for replay

Recording a bag on the robot and replaying it at your desk is the fastest way to debug perception. You get the same sensor data every run.

A minimal node

import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from geometry_msgs.msg import Twist

class Forward(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('forward')
        self.pub = self.create_publisher(Twist, '/cmd_vel', 10)
        self.create_timer(0.1, self.tick)   # 10 Hz

    def tick(self):
        msg = Twist()
        msg.linear.x = 0.2                  # m/s
        self.pub.publish(msg)

rclpy.init()
rclpy.spin(Forward())

Which version

ROS 2 releases are tied to Ubuntu releases. Humble pairs with Ubuntu 22.04 and Jazzy with Ubuntu 24.04. Which one you install on the Jetson follows from the JetPack version you flash, covered in the next module.

Crossing to the microcontroller

The microcontroller usually does not run full ROS 2. Two common bridges: micro-ROS, which makes the microcontroller appear as a real node, or a simple serial protocol with a small bridge node on the Jetson. For drones, the flight controller speaks MAVLink or uXRCE-DDS, covered in the flying path.

Simulate first

Gazebo runs a physics simulation that publishes the same topics your real robot will. Code that drives the simulated robot drives the real one with few changes. For anything that flies, simulation is where crashes should happen.

Module 5

Jetson Orin Nano

Edge computing means the robot runs its own AI on board, with no cloud round trip. Your Orin Nano is the part that does it. The work is fitting models into its memory, power and time limits.

What you have

PartOrin Nano 8GBWhy it matters
CPU6-core Arm Cortex-A78AEROS 2 nodes, planning, pre and post-processing
GPU1024-core Ampere, 32 tensor coresNeural network inference through CUDA and TensorRT
Memory8 GB, shared by CPU and GPUThe tightest limit. Model, OS and camera buffers all draw from one pool
AI performanceUp to 67 TOPS in Super ModeUnlocked by software, same hardware
Power modes15 W, 25 W, MAXN SUPERTrade speed for battery life and heat
Dev kit I/O2 MIPI CSI camera ports, USB, M.2 NVMe, 40-pin headerCameras, storage, UART and I2C to your microcontroller

Two limits to know early: the Orin Nano has no hardware video encoder and no separate deep learning accelerator, so video encoding runs on the CPU and all inference runs on the GPU.

Set it up for a robot

  1. Pick a JetPack. JetPack is NVIDIA's bundle of Linux, drivers, CUDA and TensorRT. JetPack 6.2 (Ubuntu 22.04, pairs with ROS 2 Humble) is the mature choice with the widest tool support. JetPack 7.2 (Ubuntu 24.04, pairs with Jazzy) added Orin support in mid 2026; some community tools were still catching up, so check that what you need supports it.
  2. Boot from NVMe, not an SD card. It is faster and survives vibration and power cuts better.
  3. Go headless. Turning off the desktop frees memory for your models. Work over SSH.
  4. Add swap on the NVMe as a safety net, and treat it as an alarm when it gets used.
sudo nvpmodel -q            # show current power mode
sudo nvpmodel -m <id>       # switch mode (ids are listed in /etc/nvpmodel.conf)
tegrastats                  # live RAM, GPU load, temperature, power
sudo systemctl set-default multi-user.target   # boot without the desktop

From trained model to fast engine

You already train models. Deployment on the Jetson adds one step: compiling the model for this exact GPU with TensorRT.

  1. Train in PyTorch on a workstation or in the cloud.
  2. Export to ONNX, a portable model format.
  3. Build a TensorRT engine on the Jetson itself. Engines are tied to the device and TensorRT version that built them.
  4. Load the engine in your ROS 2 node and run inference.
/usr/src/tensorrt/bin/trtexec \
  --onnx=detector.onnx \
  --saveEngine=detector.engine \
  --fp16

Precision is the main speed dial. FP32 is the default. FP16 halves the memory and usually runs much faster with almost no accuracy loss, so start there. INT8 is faster again but needs a calibration set of real images and careful accuracy checks. trtexec prints the measured latency, which is the number to put in the planner below.

Budget one: memory

Try it

Fit your stack into 8 GB

Slider values are planning estimates. Replace them with what tegrastats reports on your board. Going headless is the cheapest way to shrink the first bar.

Budget two: time

A detection is only useful if it arrives while it is still true. Total delay is capture plus pre-processing plus inference plus post-processing. While that time passes, the robot keeps moving on old information.

Try it

How far do you travel blind?

Set speed to 1 m/s for a rover and 8 m/s for a drone and compare. Do pre-processing on the GPU where you can; resizing on the CPU often costs more than people expect.

Wiring it into a machine

  • Power: feed it from a dedicated regulator off the main battery, sized for the power mode you run plus USB devices. Motors cause voltage dips that reboot a Jetson sharing their supply.
  • Link to the low-level controller: UART on the 40-pin header or USB. Header logic is 3.3 V.
  • Cameras: CSI cameras have lower latency and CPU cost. USB cameras are easier to start with.
  • Heat and vibration: keep airflow over the heatsink, mount on dampers, and secure every connector.
  • Watch it run: log tegrastats during a test. Thermal throttling looks exactly like a model that got slower for no reason.

Rolling

A ground rover forgives mistakes. It stops when the software crashes, which makes it the best first robot for a Jetson.

The architecture

LayerHardwareJobRate
Low levelMicrocontroller and motor driverRead encoders, PID on each wheel100 Hz and up
EstimationJetson CPUFuse wheel odometry and IMU (robot_localization EKF)30 to 50 Hz
MappingJetson CPUBuild a map and find the robot in it (slam_toolbox)A few Hz
NavigationJetson CPUPlan paths and avoid obstacles (Nav2), outputs /cmd_vel10 to 20 Hz
PerceptionJetson GPUDetect objects or people with a TensorRT modelCamera rate

Wheel odometry

Encoders count ticks. Distance per tick depends on the wheel size and the gear ratio. Every later stage inherits the error if this number is off.

Try it

Encoder resolution

Calibrate by driving a measured straight line and a measured number of full turns, then correct the wheel diameter and wheel spacing until odometry agrees.

Mapping and navigation

SLAM (simultaneous localization and mapping) builds a map while tracking the robot in it. With a 2D lidar, slam_toolbox does this out of the box. It publishes the map to odom transform, correcting the drift you saw in module 3.

Nav2 takes a goal pose, plans a route across the map, and follows it while steering around obstacles that were not on the map. Its output is a stream of velocity commands that your microcontroller turns into wheel speeds.

Where the GPU earns its place

Lidar navigation runs fine on the CPU. Add the GPU when the robot needs to understand what it sees: following a person, finding an object, reading a sign, or estimating depth from cameras in place of a lidar. Run that model as its own node and publish results as a topic, so navigation keeps working when perception is slow or restarting.

Build order

  • Drive the motors from the microcontroller with PWM, wheels off the ground
  • Read encoders and close a velocity PID loop on each wheel
  • Bridge the microcontroller to the Jetson and drive with a keyboard over /cmd_vel
  • Publish odometry and calibrate it on a measured course
  • Add the IMU and fuse it with an EKF
  • Add the lidar, set its transform, and build a map of one room
  • Send the robot to a goal with Nav2
  • Add a camera and a TensorRT detector as a separate node
  • Make one behavior depend on perception, such as stopping for a person

Module 6B, path

Flying

A quadcopter is unstable by nature and falls when its software stops. The design rule that follows: the Jetson never flies the aircraft. It advises the thing that does.

How a quadcopter moves

Four propellers, two spinning each way. Speed all four up to climb. Speed up one side to tilt, and the tilted thrust pushes the drone sideways. Speed up the pair that spin the same direction to rotate in place. There are no other controls, so every motion is a change in four motor speeds, updated hundreds of times a second.

Flight controller and companion computer

Flight controllerJetson (companion)
HardwareMicrocontroller board with IMU and barometer (Pixhawk class)Orin Nano on a regulated supply
SoftwarePX4 or ArduPilotLinux, ROS 2, your models
JobAttitude and rate loops, state estimation, failsafesVision, mapping, deciding where to go
If it crashesThe drone fallsThe flight controller holds position or lands

The two talk over a serial link. With PX4 and ROS 2, the bridge is uXRCE-DDS, which exposes flight controller data as ROS 2 topics. With ArduPilot, or for a simpler setup, use MAVLink through MAVROS. The Jetson sends position or velocity setpoints in offboard mode (ArduPilot calls it guided). The pilot can always take over from the radio.

Knowing where it is

The flight controller runs an EKF over its IMU, barometer, compass and GPS. Indoors or under trees GPS fails, and the Jetson can fill the gap: visual-inertial odometry from a camera and IMU, fed back to the flight controller as an external position source. This is one of the most valuable jobs an on-board GPU does.

The weight and power budget

Everything you add costs flight time twice: its weight needs more thrust, and its electronics draw power. Aim for total maximum thrust of at least twice the takeoff weight so the drone has margin to maneuver.

Try it

Hover time with a Jetson on board

Assumes 3.7 V per cell and 80% of the battery used. Hover efficiency is grams lifted per watt; find it on your motor and propeller's thrust table. Real flights land shorter than this estimate.

Safety is part of the engineering

  • Fly new code in simulation first. PX4 and ArduPilot both run software-in-the-loop against Gazebo, publishing the same topics as the real aircraft.
  • Bench test with propellers off. Always.
  • Keep a radio transmitter in hand with a mode switch that takes control back and a kill switch.
  • Set failsafes for lost link and low battery before the first flight, and test them.
  • First real offboard flights: outdoors in an open area or inside a net, low and slow.
  • Check the registration and airspace rules where you fly.

Build order

  • Fly the simulated drone by hand, then with a ROS 2 offboard script
  • Build the airframe and fly it manually with no Jetson on board
  • Tune it until position hold is solid
  • Mount the Jetson on a regulated supply and recheck weight and thrust margin
  • Connect the serial link and read flight controller topics on the Jetson
  • Run the same offboard script on the real drone: take off, hover, land
  • Add a camera and run a TensorRT model in flight, logging only
  • Close the loop: let perception set a goal, such as following a target
